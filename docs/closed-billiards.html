Polyhedral billiards

Closed billiard paths in a polyhedron

A closed billiard path is a periodic broken geodesic: straight inside each face-to-face segment, reflective at smooth boundary points, and cyclic after finitely many bounces. For ordinary paths the unfolding trick turns the billiard into a straight line in reflected copies of the polyhedron. Singular paths are what remain when that ordinary picture has a stable limit at an edge or vertex.

Ordinary closed paths

Let P be a polyhedron in Euclidean space. An ordinary billiard path is a cyclic list of boundary points x_0, x_1, ..., x_{k-1}, each lying in the relative interior of a face of P. Between consecutive points the path is a straight segment. At each bounce, the incoming and outgoing directions make equal angles with the face normal, or equivalently the tangential component of velocity is preserved while the normal component reverses.

This is the clean case. The local wall has a single tangent plane and a single normal direction, so the reflection law is deterministic. A closed path is then a periodic point of the billiard map, or, more concretely for the inventory, a finite face word together with exact hit points on those faces.

The unfolding trick

Suppose the face word is F_0 F_1 ... F_{k-1}. Instead of reflecting the moving direction at F_i, reflect the whole polyhedron across F_i. After doing this successively, the billiard path in the original polyhedron becomes a single straight segment through reflected copies. Folding those copies back recovers the original broken line.

This is why ordinary closed billiards are so computable. A proposed face word is turned into a straight-line incidence problem in unfolded space. The equal-angle law has disappeared into the geometry of the copies.

Smooth approximations give the main evidence

The most convincing reason to keep singular paths is not a formal convention. Round the edge or vertex by a smooth family of surfaces, run the ordinary reflection law there, and let the rounding radius go to zero. The bounce point and momentum jump have a stable limit.

When the path hits an edge or vertex

At an edge there are two incident faces, hence two face normals. At a vertex there are usually three or more. The phrase "the angle with the wall" no longer specifies a unique wall, so the ordinary billiard map is not defined literally at that point.

Deleting every such path loses legitimate limits of ordinary paths. Choosing one incident face adds an arbitrary tie-breaker that the polyhedron did not provide. Two limiting pictures point to a better rule: keep the limiting stationarity condition, but let all active face normals participate.

Stability under smoothing

Imagine replacing a sharp edge by a small rounded strip, or a vertex by a small rounded cap. For every positive rounding radius the boundary is smooth near the bounce, so the ordinary reflection law applies. As the radius tends to zero, the point of reflection approaches the edge or vertex, and the smooth normal approaches a direction inside the cone generated by the active face normals.

This limiting process is the primary evidence for the singular convention in the inventory. The singular trajectory is not an extra object pasted onto the theory; it is the stable endpoint of ordinary billiards on nearby smooth surfaces.

The fixed tetrahedron gives a second limit

There is another way to take the limit without changing the tetrahedron at all. Keep the sharp tetrahedron fixed and follow ordinary closed paths whose bounce points stay in face interiors while their boundary margin tends to zero. In barycentric coordinates, an ordinary face hit has exactly one zero coordinate; approaching an edge means a second coordinate tends to zero, and approaching a vertex means three coordinates do.

From this viewpoint, a singular stratum word records the closure of ordinary face words. Adjacent ordinary hits may merge onto an edge or a vertex, and the geometric broken line, length, and stationarity condition converge to the singular representative.

The normal-cone rule records both limits

On a smooth face the normal cone is one ray, so the rule is exactly ordinary reflection. On an edge it is a wedge. At a vertex it is a higher-dimensional cone. This is the same stationarity condition, just evaluated on the nonsmooth boundary of a polyhedron.

The normal-cone condition

At a boundary point x, let N_P(x) be the cone generated by the outward normals of all faces containing x. In a face interior this cone is a single ray. On an edge it is generated by the adjacent face normals. At a vertex it is generated by the normals of all incident faces.

incoming direction - outgoing direction lies in N_P(x).

Equivalently, the closed broken path is stationary for length under all variations that keep each bounce on its assigned face, edge, or vertex stratum. The momentum jump has no allowed tangential component; it is carried by the active boundary constraints.

Why this is the sensible closure

It is the smooth limit

Rounded approximations obey the ordinary reflection law. As the rounding shrinks, the limiting normal lies in the normal cone of the sharp polyhedron.

It is the fixed-polyhedron limit

Ordinary paths in the same tetrahedron can approach edges or vertices as their face-interior boundary margin tends to zero.

It agrees with ordinary billiards

On a single smooth face, the normal cone has one generator, so the condition collapses to equal-angle reflection.

It respects symmetry

Edge and vertex hits are recorded by strata such as (AD) or [D], not by an artificial choice of one incident face.

It remains computable

The same exact data model still works: period, length, barycentric points, and a cyclic stratum word.

How it appears in the inventory

In the tetrahedron visualization, ordinary rows use face words such as DABC. Singular rows use stratum words: (AD) means the edge joining vertices A and D, and [D] means the vertex D. Barycentric coordinates reveal the same distinction: one zero coordinate is a face hit, two zeros are an edge hit, and three zeros are a vertex hit.

What the convention does not claim

A physical ball striking a perfectly sharp edge need not have a deterministic outgoing direction. The inventory is making a geometric and spectral choice: include the closed broken geodesics that are stationary under the constraints of the polyhedron, especially when they arise as stable limits of ordinary billiards on smooth approximations.
