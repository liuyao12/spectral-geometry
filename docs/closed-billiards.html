Tetrahedron billiards

Closed billiard trajectories and the singular normal-cone choice

A closed billiard trajectory in a tetrahedron is easiest to believe when every bounce lands in the interior of a face: reflect the tetrahedron, unfold the path to a straight line, and fold it back. The interesting question is what to do when a limiting closed path hits an edge or a vertex, where there is no single mirror plane. The inventory uses the normal cone at that singular point. This page explains why that is the right closure of the ordinary rule.

One stationarity rule, three visible cases

On a smooth face, the normal-cone rule is just the ordinary equal-angle reflection law. On an edge or vertex, the same rule keeps the allowed impulse inside the cone generated by the active face normals. Nothing extra is chosen by hand.

Ordinary closed trajectories

Let P be the regular tetrahedron, with faces F_A, F_B, F_C, and F_D opposite the vertices A, B, C, and D. An ordinary billiard trajectory is a cyclic polygonal path x_0, x_1, ..., x_{k-1} on the boundary of P, with each x_i lying in the relative interior of one face. Between bounces the particle moves along a straight segment. At each face it obeys the familiar mirror rule: the incoming and outgoing directions make equal angles with the face normal.

The unfolding trick packages this rule perfectly. If the face word is DABC, reflect the tetrahedron successively across those faces. The broken billiard path in the original tetrahedron becomes a straight segment through reflected copies. The path is closed when, after folding the copies back, the final direction and position return to the start. This is why the inventory can store an ordinary orbit by a face word, exact barycentric hit points, and an exact length.

Where the smooth rule breaks

Edges and vertices are not smooth reflecting walls. At an edge there are two incident faces and hence two face normals. At a vertex there are three. If a closed polygonal path lands exactly on such a stratum, the phrase "the angle with the wall" stops being well-defined.

One tempting response is to delete every such path. That is too harsh: ordinary closed paths can move toward the boundary of the face, and their limiting hit point can lie on an edge or vertex. Another response is to pick one of the incident faces and pretend the impact happened there. That is too arbitrary: the edge itself does not choose between its incident faces, and a tetrahedral symmetry may exchange them. A spectral catalogue should not depend on a tie-breaker that the geometry did not provide.

The normal-cone condition

The replacement is simple. At a boundary point x, let N_P(x) be the cone generated by the outward normals of all faces containing x. In a face interior this cone is a single ray. On an edge it is the two-dimensional cone spanned by the two adjacent face normals. At a vertex it is generated by the three adjacent face normals.

incoming direction - outgoing direction lies in N_P(x).

Up to the sign convention for inward versus outward normals, this is exactly the usual reflection law on a smooth face: the tangential component of velocity is unchanged, while the normal component reverses. The point of the formula is that it still makes sense when there is more than one active normal.

There is also a variational reading. Fix the strata where the bounces occur, and vary the hit points along those strata. A closed broken path is stationary for total length precisely when the first variation vanishes along every allowed tangent direction. Equivalently, the momentum jump at each bounce has no tangential component and is supported by the active constraints, which is the normal-cone condition above.

Why this is the sensible closure

It agrees with the ordinary rule

On a single face, the normal cone has only one generator, so the condition collapses to equal-angle reflection. We are not replacing ordinary billiards; we are extending its endpoint condition to the nonsmooth boundary.

It is stable under limits

If a sequence of ordinary stationary paths converges to a path whose hit lies on an edge, the limiting momentum jump cannot acquire an arbitrary tangential component. It lands in the cone generated by the normals of the faces that have become active.

It respects symmetry

Edge and vertex hits are recorded by strata such as (AD) or [D], not by an artificial choice of one incident face. This keeps tetrahedral symmetries visible instead of baking in a coordinate accident.

It remains computable

The same exact data model still works: period, length, barycentric points, and a cyclic word. The only difference is that the word is a stratum word rather than a pure face word.

How it appears in the inventory

In the visualization, ordinary rows use face words such as DABC. Singular rows use stratum words: (AD) means the edge joining vertices A and D, and [D] means the vertex D. Barycentric coordinates reveal the same distinction: one zero coordinate is a face hit, two zeros are an edge hit, and three zeros are a vertex hit.

Singular representatives are shown folded in the 3D viewer. That is a feature, not a missing trick: a face-word unfolding requires a unique sequence of mirror faces, while an edge or vertex hit deliberately keeps all active faces in play.

What the convention does not claim

A physical ball striking a perfectly sharp edge need not have a deterministic outgoing direction. The inventory is making a geometric and spectral choice instead: include the closed broken geodesics that are stationary under the constraints of the polyhedron, and include them in a way that is continuous from the ordinary billiard law. The normal cone is exactly the object that records those constraints without adding a hidden preference.
